# **Abstract**

Sustainable investing now directs trillions of dollars, but it is still unclear whether this money changes how firms behave or only how their shares are priced. We review 35 theoretical and empirical studies and organize them around two questions. First, does sustainable investing change a firm’s cost of capital? It does, but the effect is modest. Excluded and carbon-intensive stocks earn higher returns (Hong & Kacperczyk, 2009; Bolton & Kacperczyk, 2021). Even so, calibrated estimates suggest that divestment barely moves the cost of capital in diversified equity markets (Berk & van Binsbergen, 2025), and some of the recent outperformance of green stocks came from fund flows and rising climate concern rather than lower risk (Pástor, Stambaugh, & Taylor, 2022; van der Beck, 2021). Second, when the cost of capital does change, do firms respond? Here the evidence is weaker. Hartzmark and Shue (2026) find that cheaper capital does little for green firms, while costlier capital makes brown firms pollute more. Sustainable funds also tend to pick firms that already behave well rather than improve them (Heath, Macciocchi, Michaely, & Ringgenberg, 2023). Shareholder engagement has a better record, and several studies link it to real cuts in emissions (Dimson, Karakas, & Li, 2015; Azar, Duro, Kadach, & Ormazabal, 2021; Kahn, Matsusaka, & Shu, 2023). We conclude that sustainable investing moves prices more reliably than it moves behavior, and that the evidence for real effects is generally stronger for engagement than for exit.

# **1. Introduction**

Over the past decade, sustainable investing has moved from a niche strategy into the mainstream. University endowments have faced campaigns to divest from fossil fuels. The largest asset managers have promised to use their votes on climate issues. A growing share of mutual fund money now passes through some kind of environmental, social, and governance (ESG) screen. What remains unsettled is whether any of this changes what companies actually do. When investors act on their values, do firms pollute less, or does the market simply reprice their shares while operations carry on as before?

The distinction matters for welfare. If sustainable investing mainly changes prices, investors get portfolios that match their values, and brown firms may pay slightly more for capital, but the pollution or labor abuses that motivated the strategy continue. If it changes behavior, capital markets become a real lever for social change and can work alongside regulation. Asset managers increasingly market their funds on the second claim. Whether the evidence supports that claim is an empirical question, and it is the question this survey addresses.

We think the clearest way to approach this question is to split it into two links. The first link is financial: does divestment, ESG portfolio tilting, or a bank’s decision to stop lending raise a targeted firm’s cost of capital? The second link is operational: if the cost of capital changes, does the firm change its investment, emissions, or governance in response? A strategy can succeed on the first link and fail on the second. We also ask a related question about mechanism. Is a firm more likely to change when investors sell its shares (exit) or when they keep the shares and press management for change (voice)?

Our anchor paper is Hartzmark and Shue (2026). They measure what they call impact elasticity, which is how much a firm’s environmental impact changes when its cost of capital changes. They find that the response is lopsided. Cutting green firms’ financing costs does very little, but raising brown firms’ financing costs makes them noticeably worse. If that result holds, the most common sustainable strategy, moving money from brown firms to green ones, works against its own goal. Much of this survey asks whether the rest of the literature supports that conclusion.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 explains how we selected and grouped the studies. Section 3 reviews the theory behind both links. Section 4 asks whether sustainable investing moves the cost of capital, covering divestment, ESG integration, and bank lending. Section 5 asks whether changes in the cost of capital change firm behavior. Section 6 compares divestment with engagement directly, and Section 7 reviews current industry practice and investor beliefs. Section 8 brings the evidence together, Section 9 discusses limitations and open questions, and Section 10 concludes. Throughout, we track the three strategies named in our title. Divestment and bank exit are covered mainly in Sections 4.1, 4.3, 5.5, and 6; ESG integration in Sections 4.2, 5.3, and 5.4; and engagement in Section 6. Section 8.2 compares the three directly, and Table A1 in the Appendix summarizes all 35 studies.

Our reading of the evidence is that the first link holds but is weak, and the second is weaker still. Where sustainable investing has clearly changed corporate behavior, it has usually done so through engagement rather than through prices.

# **2. Scope and Approach**

This is a survey, so our contribution lies in how we select and organize existing work rather than in new data. We focused on studies published in leading finance and economics journals, including the Journal of Finance, the Journal of Financial Economics, the Review of Financial Studies, the Journal of Political Economy, the Review of Finance, and Management Science. We also included a few recent working papers from the NBER, SSRN, CEPR, and ECGI series when they speak directly to our question and have drawn wide attention, such as Hartzmark and Shue (2026) and Kahn, Matsusaka, and Shu (2023). In total we discuss 35 studies published between 2001 and 2026. We used generative AI to help locate and verify studies and to draft and format the paper; the Statement on the Use of Generative AI at the end of the paper describes this use in detail.

We sorted each study by the part of the argument it informs. Some are theoretical and bear on both links. A second group tests whether sustainable investing affects prices, returns, or financing costs, which is the first link. A third group asks whether financing conditions change what firms do, which is the second. The last group compares divestment and engagement head-on or reports what investors say they do. Some papers fit more than one category. Kacperczyk and Peydró (2022), for example, document both a cut in bank credit and the lack of environmental improvement that followed it. In such cases we discuss the paper where it adds the most and refer back to it elsewhere.

We also paid attention to how each study identifies its effects. Correlations between ESG ownership and firm outcomes are easy to find but hard to interpret, because investors may simply buy firms that were already improving. We therefore give more weight to studies that rely on natural experiments or plausibly exogenous shocks, such as shifts in the political control of pension funds, bank coal exit policies, or changes in the supply of socially responsible capital. Where a result rests mainly on correlation, we say so.

# **3. Theoretical Background**

The theory starts from a simple idea. If some investors refuse to hold a stock, the remaining investors must hold more of it and carry more of its risk, so they demand a higher return. Heinkel, Kraus, and Zechner (2001) built an equilibrium model around this idea. In their model, green investors exclude polluting firms, which shrinks those firms’ investor base and raises their cost of capital. If the penalty is large enough, some polluters find it cheaper to clean up than to keep paying it. The catch is the size of the penalty. Under reasonable parameter values, more than 20 percent of investors would have to be green before any polluter chose to reform, while the evidence at the time suggested that green investors controlled at most 10 percent of invested funds. The model also treats reform as all-or-nothing, which rules out the gradual improvements we usually observe in practice.

Pástor, Stambaugh, and Taylor (2021) extend this logic to a market where investors can tilt their portfolios toward green assets instead of simply excluding brown ones. Investors in their model care about returns but also get satisfaction from holding green assets. As a result, green assets trade at higher prices and offer lower expected returns. Green assets also hedge climate risk, which pushes their expected returns down further. One implication is easy to miss. Green assets can still beat brown assets for a period if concern about climate rises faster than investors expected. A lower expected return does not guarantee a lower realized return in any given sample, and this point matters when we read the empirical work in Section 4.

Pedersen, Fitzgibbons, and Pomorski (2021) give an ESG score two jobs. It can carry information about a firm’s future profits, and it can matter to investors for its own sake. They trace out an “ESG-efficient frontier” that shows the best Sharpe ratio an investor can achieve at each level of portfolio ESG. Whether high-ESG stocks should earn depends on which job the score is doing. If ESG mainly signals strong fundamentals that the market has not yet priced, high-ESG stocks can earn more. If investors mainly value ESG as a preference, those stocks should earn less. Because the model allows either outcome, it helps explain why empirical studies disagree about the sign of the relationship between ESG and returns.

Broccardo, Hart, and Zingales (2022) compare exit and voice directly. Investors in their model can sell shares in a firm that causes social harm, or they can keep the shares and vote to change the firm’s policy. Voice wins as long as most investors are even slightly socially minded, because those investors can form a majority and vote the change through. Exit works only if nearly everyone is strongly socially minded, since a few divestors barely move the price. Exit also invites free riding. An investor who keeps the stock benefits from any price effect created by those who sell, so the individual incentive to divest is weak. Voting for a better policy does not suffer from this problem when investors are well diversified.

Edmans, Levit, and Schneemeier (2025) look at divestment from the investor’s side. Their responsible investor cares about both returns and social impact but cannot commit in advance to future trades. Excluding a brown firm starves it of capital and limits its output, but it gives the firm no reason to reform. Investing only after a firm reform does reward reform, but it also helps the firm grow, which can raise its total externalities. In their model, reform happens only when the investor’s social concern is moderate. An investor who cares too much will keep excluding the firm even after it shows signs of reform, so the promise to reinvest is not credible and the firm has no reason to change. This result foreshadows Section 5: starving brown firms of capital can make them smaller without making them cleaner.

# **4. Does Sustainable Investing Move the Cost of Capital?**

This is the easier of the two links to test, and the evidence here is more consistent than the evidence we review in Section 5.

## ***4.1 Exclusion and Stock Returns***

The best-known evidence on exclusion comes from “sin stocks.” Hong and Kacperczyk (2009) study alcohol, tobacco, and gaming companies, which many institutions avoid because of social norms or legal restrictions. Pension funds hold fewer of these stocks than mutual funds and hedge funds do, and analysts cover them less. Consistent with this neglect, sin stocks earn higher expected returns than comparable firms. The authors attribute the gap to norm-driven avoidance and to higher litigation risk.

Bolton and Kacperczyk (2021) find a similar pattern for carbon emissions. U.S. firms with higher total emissions, and firms whose emissions are growing, earn higher stock returns after controlling for size, book-to-market, and other standard predictors. The authors read this premium as compensation that investors demand for carrying carbon risk. They also find that institutional investors screen out firms with high emission intensity in a few salient industries.

Chava (2014) extends this evidence from equity to debt. Firms with environmental concerns have lower institutional ownership, and fewer banks join their loan syndicates. Investors also expect significantly higher returns on the stocks of heavy polluters. Exclusionary investing and environmentally sensitive lending therefore appear to raise both the cost of equity and the cost of debt for these firms. Together, these three papers support the basic prediction of Heinkel et al. (2001): firms that investors and lenders avoid end up paying more for capital.

It helps to be precise about what these return results mean. A firm’s cost of equity is the return investors expect to earn on its stock. When investors shun a stock, its price falls until the expected return is high enough to attract the remaining buyers, so a higher expected return for investors is a higher cost of capital for the firm. Measuring this is difficult, because realized returns are noisy and can differ from expected returns for years at a time. Studies therefore either average realized returns over long samples, as Hong and Kacperczyk (2009) and Bolton and Kacperczyk (2021) do, or infer the cost of capital from stock prices and analyst forecasts, as Chava (2014) and El Ghoul et al. (2011) do.

How large is this effect? Berk and van Binsbergen (2025) argue that it is very small. They show that the change in a divested firm’s cost of capital depends on three things: the share of capital held by socially conscious investors, the share of firms being excluded, and how closely the excluded firms’ returns move with the rest of the market. Calibrated to current conditions, divestment changes the cost of capital by only a fraction of a basis point. When the authors examine firms entering and leaving ESG indexes, they find no measurable change in the cost of capital. By their estimate, socially conscious investors would need to hold more than 80 percent of the market before divestment could meaningfully affect investment decisions.

El Ghoul, Guedhami, Kwok, and Mishra (2011) reach a more encouraging result with a broader measure. Firms with stronger corporate social responsibility (CSR) scores, especially on employee relations, environmental policy, and product strategy, have a significantly lower cost of equity. Involvement in tobacco or nuclear power raises it. One way to reconcile this with Berk and van Binsbergen (2025) is that the two papers measure different things. Binary exclusion affects only the demand for a stock, while a broad CSR score may also capture lower litigation, regulatory, and reputational risk. Lower risk of that kind would reduce the cost of capital regardless of investor tastes.

## ***4.2 ESG Integration and the Debate over Returns***

ESG integration begins with investor demand, and there is good causal evidence that this demand is real. Hartzmark and Sussman (2019) use Morningstar’s introduction of sustainability ratings for mutual funds in 2016 as a natural experiment. Funds labeled as low in sustainability suffered net outflows of more than $12 billion, while funds labeled as high in sustainability attracted net inflows of more than $24 billion. Investors appear to have treated sustainability as a signal of future performance, yet the authors find no evidence that high-sustainability funds outperformed low-sustainability funds. Flows of this size can move prices, which is the subject of the next two studies.

If green firms have a lower cost of capital, their stocks should earn lower returns. Yet green stocks did well over much of the past decade. Pástor, Stambaugh, and Taylor (2022) take up this puzzle in “Dissecting Green Returns.” U.S. green stocks did outperform brown stocks, and German green government bonds outperformed otherwise identical conventional bonds. The authors show that this outperformance came from an unexpectedly sharp rise in climate concern rather than from high expected returns. Once they account for that shock, the expected return on green stocks is still lower than on brown stocks, as their 2021 model predicts.

Van der Beck (2021) offers a different explanation. Using a structural model of price impact, he estimates that each dollar flowing into a representative ESG portfolio raises the market value of ESG stocks by about 80 cents. Over his sample, these flows added about 1.9 percent per year to ESG returns, and without them ESG stocks would not have outperformed the market. This result challenges a view implicit in Berk and van Binsbergen (2025), namely that shifting sustainable capital barely moves prices. It also carries a warning. If flows drove ESG returns, those returns could reverse when the flows slow down.

Measurement is a further problem. Gibson Brandon, Krueger, and Schmidt (2021) document that rating agencies often give the same firm very different ESG scores. The agencies disagree about which categories to include, how to measure each one, and how much weight each should get. This disagreement is itself priced: stocks with more disagreement earn higher returns, mainly because of disagreement on the environmental dimension. Part of any measured link between ESG and returns may therefore reflect noise in the ratings rather than sustainability itself.

A related question is whether investors who say they integrate ESG actually do so. Gibson Brandon, Glossner, Krueger, Matos, and Steffen (2022) study signatories of the Principles for Responsible Investment (PRI), a global initiative in which investors commit to responsible investing. Outside the United States, signatories hold portfolios with better ESG scores than non-signatories. U.S. signatories, however, hold portfolios with ESG scores that are no better, and in some tests worse, than those of investors who made no commitment. The gap appears to be driven partly by underperforming investors who sign up to attract fund flows, which is consistent with greenwashing. If many committed investors do not actually tilt their portfolios, the pricing effects of integration will be smaller than headline asset totals suggest.

## ***4.3 Bank Lending***

Sustainable finance also works through banks. Kacperczyk and Peydró (2022) treat banks’ public decarbonization commitments as a shift in lenders’ green preferences. High-emission firms that had borrowed from committing banks later received significantly less bank credit. The effect comes from a reduced supply of credit rather than from banks repricing borrower risk, and it is followed by lower debt, leverage, assets, and investment at the affected firms. Banks shifted credit from brown firms to green ones instead of lending to brown firms to finance cleaner technology, a point we return to in Section 5.

Green and Vallée (2025) study bank policies to stop financing coal. A one-standard-deviation increase in a firm’s exposure to these policies reduces its annual debt issuance by about 20 percent, and affected firms find few substitute lenders. Coal plants owned by exposed firms are more likely to close, and the authors estimate that these policies have avoided about 0.62 gigatons of CO2-equivalent emissions. Bank credit is concentrated and hard to replace, which may explain why exit has much stronger effects here than in public equity markets.

Overall, sustainable investing does move financing costs. The effect is clearest for carbon-intensive and stigmatized industries and for firms that depend on a small number of lenders. In diversified stock markets it is usually small, and part of the measured return gap may reflect fund flows or rating noise. Whether these price effects change what firms do is a separate question, and we turn to it next.

# **5. Does the Cost of Capital Change Firm Behavior?**

Even when the cost of capital changes, firms may not respond the way investors hope. We start with our anchor paper and then turn to related work on financial constraints, fund selection, and managerial incentives.

## ***5.1 Impact Elasticity***

Hartzmark and Shue (2026) define impact elasticity as the change in a firm’s environmental impact caused by a change in its cost of capital. Their main finding is that this elasticity is asymmetric. Lowering green firms’ financing costs produces almost no further improvement, which makes sense because these firms have little left to clean up. Raising brown firms’ financing costs, on the other hand, makes their impact significantly worse. In the authors’ framing, the strategy makes brown firms more brown without making green firms more green. They also criticize the common practice of judging firms by percentage cuts in emissions. Because green firms emit so little, a trivial cut can look like a large percentage reduction, while brown firms get weak incentives to make the large absolute cuts that matter most. If these estimates hold up, the standard brown-to-green reallocation is counterproductive.

This result sits uneasily with the original theory. Heinkel et al. (2001) assumed that a higher cost of capital pushes a brown firm toward reform once the penalty exceeds the cost of cleaning up. One reading of Hartzmark and Shue’s evidence, consistent with the work on financial constraints discussed next, is that the penalty is usually too small to make full reform worthwhile, which is what Heinkel et al.’s own calibration implied. The firm then absorbs the higher cost instead of reforming, and its tighter budget can leave its operations dirtier than before.

## ***5.2 Financial Constraints***

Earlier work on financial constraints points in the same direction. Hong, Kubik, and Scheinkman (2012) model a firm that decides how much to invest in physical capital and how much to spend on “corporate goodness,” subject to a financing constraint. Less constrained firms spend more on goodness, and the data agree: firms with more financial slack have higher social responsibility scores. Xu and Kim (2022) test the idea with plant-level pollution data. Using three quasi-natural experiments that change firms’ financial resources for reasons unrelated to the environment, they show that relaxing financial constraints reduces toxic releases. The effect is stronger when regulatory enforcement is weaker and when managers face short-term earnings pressure.

Read alongside Hartzmark and Shue (2026), these papers suggest a fairly mechanical explanation. Firms weigh spending on abatement against other uses of scarce cash. Making capital more expensive for a constrained brown firm tightens that trade-off and cuts abatement. Making capital cheaper for an unconstrained green firm changes little. That is the asymmetry Hartzmark and Shue report.

## ***5.3 Selection versus Treatment***

Heath, Macciocchi, Michaely, and Ringgenberg (2023) raise a different concern, which is selection. They study socially responsible investment (SRI) funds and the firms these funds hold. SRI funds do pick firms with better environmental and social records. Using shocks to the amount of SRI capital, however, the authors find no evidence that holding a firm improves its behavior afterward. They call this “impact washing.” The funds are not misrepresenting what they hold, since their holdings really are cleaner than average, but the claim that they make those firms cleaner is not supported. This fits Hartzmark and Shue’s (2026) result for green firms: even if a green firm’s cost of capital falls when an SRI fund buys its shares, the firm has little reason to change.

## ***5.4 Does ESG Integration Change Firms?***

Two further studies ask what integration does in practice. Atta-Darkua, Glossner, Krueger, and Matos (2025) study institutional investors that join climate-related investor initiatives. These investors can lower the carbon footprint of their portfolios in two ways: by reweighting toward lower-emitting firms, or by engaging with portfolio companies to cut their emissions. The authors find that reweighting is the dominant strategy, especially among investors based in countries with carbon pricing, and they find little evidence of engagement, even after the 2015 Paris Agreement. Climate-conscious investors do shift toward firms that are beginning to earn green revenues, but they do not direct capital toward firms that develop climate patents. The authors conclude that these initiatives mostly green investors’ portfolios rather than the economy.

Cohen, Gurun, and Nguyen (2026) point to a deeper mismatch between ESG scores and real innovation. Using U.S. patent data, they show that traditional energy firms produce more green patents than other firms, and patents of significantly higher quality. Yet these same firms receive lower ESG scores and smaller ESG fund flows, and they are not rewarded for additional green innovation. An integration strategy that tilts away from energy firms may therefore withdraw capital from some of the firms doing the most to develop clean technology. Combined with Heath et al. (2023), these studies suggest that integration, as currently practiced, mainly changes what investors hold rather than what firms do.

## ***5.5 When Financial Pressure Backfires and When It Works***

Divestment can also backfire. In Davies and Van Wesep’s (2018) model, a large divestment campaign lowers the target firm’s share price. Under standard pay contracts, that lower price can end up rewarding the managers who attracted the campaign, which gives executives a reason to increase rather than reduce the harm their firms cause. Divesting investors also earn lower and riskier returns than investors who stay, so over time they control a shrinking share of total wealth and their influence fades.

Gantchev, Giannetti, and Li (2022) show when the threat of exit does work. They study firms after environmental or social incidents. Actual divestment after these incidents is modest. Still, firms with a one-standard-deviation higher share of ESG-conscious institutional owners cut greenhouse gas emissions by 36.5 percent more, and raise their environmental and social scores by 7.2 percent more, than similar firms. This happens only when managers’ pay is tied to the stock price. The selling itself is not what changes behavior. What matters is managers’ expectation that continued bad conduct will lead investors to sell, at a personal cost to them. That is a much narrower condition than simply raising a firm’s average cost of capital.

Not all of the evidence on divestment is negative. Rohleder, Wilkens, and Zink (2022) identify decarbonization trades in mutual fund holdings, that is, sales of carbon-intensive stocks made to lower a fund’s carbon footprint. These sales push the targeted stocks’ prices down over several periods, and divested firms whose share prices fell went on to reduce their carbon emissions relative to firms that were not divested. Their results support the hope that a critical mass of divesting investors can cut emissions. They are harder to square with Hartzmark and Shue (2026) and Kahn et al. (2023). One possible reconciliation is that divestment works only when selling is concentrated enough to move the share price noticeably, which would make it most effective through the same managerial-incentive channel that Gantchev et al. (2022) identify. Testing this directly is a useful task for future research.

The two banking studies from Section 4 illustrate, within a single channel, when financial pressure works and when it does not. In Kacperczyk and Peydró (2022), brown firms that lose credit shrink their debt, assets, and investment, but their hard environmental scores do not improve, and the authors find only evidence consistent with greenwashing. Lending fell, but environmental performance did not get better. In Green and Vallée (2025), by contrast, coal exit policies led to plant closures and large reductions in emissions. One explanation is targeting. The coal policies hit a specific type of asset with few alternative sources of financing, so the shock was large and hard to avoid. Broad decarbonization pledges left brown borrowers room to adjust their reporting rather than their operations.

The evidence for the second link is therefore thin. The newest and most direct study suggests that the standard reallocation strategy may make things worse (Hartzmark & Shue, 2026). Work on financial constraints helps explain why the effect is lopsided (Hong, Kubik, & Scheinkman, 2012; Xu & Kim, 2022). Fund-level evidence suggests that sustainable funds mostly pick good firms rather than make firms good (Heath et al., 2023), and that climate-conscious investors mostly reweight their portfolios rather than push firms to cut emissions (Atta-Darkua et al., 2025). Bank-level evidence shows that a real cut in credit need not lead to cleaner operations (Kacperczyk & Peydró, 2022). The cases where behavior does change have something in common: the financial pressure is large, targeted, and tied to something managers care about directly (Gantchev et al., 2022; Rohleder et al., 2022; Green & Vallée, 2025).

# **6. Divestment versus Engagement**

If the price channel is this fragile, does engagement do better? Broccardo, Hart, and Zingales (2022) predict that it should. This section reviews the empirical tests.

Dimson, Karakas, and Li (2015) provide the classic evidence. They use proprietary data on environmental, social, and governance engagements between a specialist asset manager and U.S. public companies from 1999 to 2009. When an engagement succeeds, meaning the firm makes the requested change, the firm’s stock earns an abnormal return of 4.4 percent over the following year. Unsuccessful engagements produce no significant abnormal return. Successful engagements are also followed by better accounting performance, stronger governance, and more institutional ownership. Engagements succeed more often when several investors work together and when the target has a reputation to protect and the capacity to change.

Flammer (2015) provides cleaner causal evidence on a more formal kind of voice: shareholder proposals on corporate social responsibility. She compares proposals that narrowly pass with proposals that narrowly fail, which is close to random assignment. Adopting a close-call CSR proposal leads to positive announcement returns and better accounting performance. Return on assets rises by about 0.7 to 0.8 percentage points over the next two fiscal years, and labor productivity and sales growth also increase. Kahn et al. (2023), discussed below, find that persuasion matters more than confrontational voting, but Flammer’s results show that when a vote does pass, it can change both firm value and operations.

Azar, Duro, Kadach, and Ormazabal (2021) study the “Big Three” index fund managers: BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street. Firms in which the Big Three hold larger stakes have lower carbon emissions afterward, and the relationship has grown stronger since these managers publicly committed to ESG stewardship. The Big Three focus their engagement on large, high-emitting firms in which they own significant stakes. This setting is useful because an index fund cannot sell a stock that remains in its index. Whatever influence it has must come from voting and engagement, not from the threat of exit.

Two studies of hedge fund activism trace these effects down to individual plants. Naaraayanan, Sachdeva, and Sharma (2026) find that after environmental activist campaigns, targeted plants cut toxic releases, greenhouse gas emissions, and cancer-causing pollution. Air quality improves within one mile of these plants, and the improvement comes from higher spending on abatement equipment. The gains have a cost, however, since the targeted firms’ profitability and operating income decline. Akey and Appel (2019) use plant-level data from the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) to study hedge fund activism more broadly. They find that activism is followed by a 17 percent drop in emissions at targeted plants. Most of the drop comes from lower production rather than new abatement, but emissions per unit of output still fall by about 8 percent. A reduction that comes mainly from producing less is still a real reduction. It is a weaker kind of success than cleaner production, though, and it leaves open what happens to the output the plant no longer makes.

Engagement does not have to replace exit, and the best evidence suggests the two work together. As discussed in Section 5, Gantchev et al. (2022) find that the threat of exit makes engagement more effective when managers’ pay depends on the stock price. Edmans, Levit, and Schneemeier (2025) reach a similar conclusion from theory: withholding capital until a firm reforms can induce change that permanent exclusion cannot, but only if the investor’s promise to reinvest is believable. In both cases, exit works best as a conditional threat rather than a final verdict.

The most direct test comes from Kahn, Matsusaka, and Shu (2023). They classify U.S. public pension funds as green or non-green according to the political party that controls them. Election results that shift control of a fund, along with rebalancing triggered by returns on the fund’s non-equity holdings, produce changes in green ownership that are plausibly unrelated to a firm’s own emissions path. Firms reduce greenhouse gas emissions when green funds’ ownership rises. When ownership by non-green funds rises, emissions stay the same or increase. Using proxy votes, shareholder proposals, and the behavior of activist pension funds, the authors trace the effect to engagement, and specifically to persuading management, rather than to confrontational voting. They find no sign that firms simply sold their dirtiest facilities to look cleaner. Their conclusion is pointed. Divestment can give up the very influence that engagement depends on, and it may therefore lead to higher emissions.

Why does engagement work when exit does not? The studies point to three features. First, engagement carries information. Investors can explain to management why an issue matters, and Kahn et al. (2023) find that persuasion, rather than voting pressure, accounts for most of the effect. Second, engagement can solve coordination problems. Dimson et al. (2015) find that collaboration among investors raises success rates on environmental and social issues, which matches the coalition logic in Broccardo et al. (2022). Third, engagement works through reputation, since targets with more reputational exposure are more likely to comply (Dimson et al., 2015). None of these channels depends on moving the firm’s cost of capital, which may be why engagement succeeds where price-based strategies struggle.

These studies cover very different investors: a specialist engagement fund, shareholders voting on CSR proposals, passive index managers, hedge funds, and public pension funds. In each case, engagement is linked to real changes in emissions or governance. That pattern is far more consistent than anything we found for the price channel, and it matches the prediction of Broccardo et al. (2022).

# **7. Current Industry Practice and Investor Beliefs**

## ***7.1 How Sustainable Investing Is Implemented in Practice***

In practice, sustainable investing is commonly implemented through ESG integration, portfolio reweighting or exclusion, shareholder engagement, and lending restrictions on high-carbon firms. These approaches differ in how they are expected to create impact. Some mainly change what investors hold, while others try to influence firms more directly.

One common approach is to make portfolios greener by shifting capital toward firms with better ESG scores or lower emissions. Evidence from PRI signatories and climate-focused investors suggests that much of this activity happens through portfolio reweighting rather than direct engagement with firms. In other words, investors may reduce the carbon footprint of their own portfolios without necessarily reducing the emissions of the companies themselves.

Another approach is engagement, where investors remain shareholders and use voting, meetings with management, and shareholder proposals to push for change. Large investors such as the Big Three have increasingly used this form of stewardship, while banks have also adopted climate-related lending and sector-exit policies. The evidence reviewed earlier suggests that these strategies can have stronger real effects when investors have direct influence or when firms cannot easily replace the financing they lose.

Overall, current industry practice reflects the same distinction found in the academic literature: portfolio reweighting mainly changes which firms investors hold, while engagement and targeted financing policies are more directly aimed at changing what firms do.

## ***7.2 What Investors Say***

Surveys of institutional investors provide additional evidence on how practitioners view these different strategies. Krueger, Sautner, and Starks (2020) survey 439 institutional investors, including chief investment officers and ESG specialists. Most respondents believe that climate risks are already financially material or will become so within a few years, with regulatory risk viewed as especially important. Larger, longer-horizon, and more ESG-focused investors tend to prefer engagement and risk management to divestment. Many also believe that current stock prices do not fully reflect climate risk, although they generally do not view the mispricing as very large.

Edmans, Gosling, and Jenter (2025) survey 509 equity portfolio managers at both sustainable and conventional funds. Environmental and social performance affects stock selection, engagement, or voting decisions for more than three quarters of respondents, including nearly two thirds of managers at conventional funds. Nevertheless, financial returns remain the dominant objective even at sustainable funds, and relatively few managers are willing to sacrifice returns for better environmental or social outcomes, often because of fiduciary-duty concerns. This reluctance may help explain why the price effects documented in the earlier sections are often modest: if few investors are willing to accept lower expected returns, relatively little capital is permanently withheld from brown firms.

Edmans and Kacperczyk (2022) provide a useful framework for interpreting these practices. They identify three broad motives for sustainable finance: the effect of sustainability issues on firm value and risk, investors’ non-financial objectives, and the satisfaction investors receive from holding particular assets. These motives need not imply the same objective. An investor who integrates ESG information because it improves risk-adjusted returns may not be trying to change corporate behavior at all. The evidence in this survey is therefore most relevant for investors and asset managers who claim that their investment choices create real-world environmental impact, rather than simply improving portfolio characteristics.

# **8. Synthesis**

We can now return to the question in our title.

## ***8.1 Answering the Two Questions***

On the first link, the answer is a qualified yes. Exclusion raises the cost of capital for carbon-intensive and stigmatized firms (Hong & Kacperczyk, 2009; Chava, 2014; Bolton & Kacperczyk, 2021), and bank exit can sharply restrict financing when credit is hard to replace (Kacperczyk & Peydró, 2022; Green & Vallée, 2025). In diversified equity markets, however, the effect is small (Berk & van Binsbergen, 2025), and part of the recent return gap reflects flows and changing climate concern rather than a lasting change in risk (van der Beck, 2021; Pástor, Stambaugh, & Taylor, 2022).

On the second link, the answer is mostly no, and in some cases worse than no. Hartzmark and Shue (2026) find that the standard reallocation strategy barely helps green firms and makes brown firms dirtier. The financial-constraints literature helps explain why (Hong, Kubik, & Scheinkman, 2012; Xu & Kim, 2022). Sustainable funds mostly select good firms rather than improve them (Heath et al., 2023), climate-conscious investors mostly reweight rather than engage (Atta-Darkua et al., 2025), and cutting bank credit to brown firms did not improve their environmental performance (Kacperczyk & Peydró, 2022). Behavior changes only under narrower conditions: when managers’ pay is tied to the share price (Gantchev et al., 2022), when selling is heavy enough to push the share price down (Rohleder et al., 2022), or when a targeted financing shock is hard to escape (Green & Vallée, 2025).

On mechanism, the evidence favors voice. Engagement by a specialist fund, index managers, hedge funds, and pension funds is repeatedly linked to lower emissions and better governance (Dimson et al., 2015; Azar et al., 2021; Akey & Appel, 2019; Naaraayanan et al., 2026; Kahn et al., 2023), and CSR proposals that narrowly pass improve firm performance (Flammer, 2015). Broccardo et al. (2022) explain why: voice needs only a majority within one firm, while exit needs most of the market to take part. The evidence also suggests that exit and voice work best together. A credible threat to sell makes engagement more effective (Gantchev et al., 2022), provided the investor’s promise to return after reform is believable (Edmans, Levit, & Schneemeier, 2025).

## ***8.2 Comparing Divestment, Integration, and Engagement***

Our title names three strategies, so it is worth comparing them directly. Divestment is the bluntest tool. Its price effects are clearest in narrow settings such as sin stocks and coal finance, but in diversified equity markets its effect on the cost of capital is small, and on its own it gives firms no reason to reform. Its real effects come from a large, targeted financing shock, as in Green and Vallée (2025), from selling heavy enough to depress the share price (Rohleder et al., 2022), or from the threat of exit rather than exit itself (Gantchev et al., 2022).

ESG integration sits in the middle. Firms with stronger ESG profiles have a lower cost of equity (El Ghoul et al., 2011), and flows into ESG funds have shaped recent returns (Hartzmark & Sussman, 2019; van der Beck, 2021). But integration mostly rewards firms that are already green, which is exactly where Hartzmark and Shue (2026) find impact elasticity to be close to zero. It also depends on ESG ratings that frequently disagree (Gibson Brandon et al., 2021), so two investors can tilt toward different firms while believing they follow the same strategy. Investors who commit to integration do not always follow through (Gibson Brandon et al., 2022), and those who decarbonize their portfolios mostly do so by reweighting rather than by changing firms (Atta-Darkua et al., 2025). ESG scores can even penalize the energy firms that produce much of the economy’s green innovation (Cohen et al., 2026). We found no study showing that a firm improves simply because more ESG-tilted portfolios hold its shares.

Among the strategies reviewed, engagement shows the most consistent evidence of real changes in firm behavior, and some of that evidence comes from the strongest research designs in this literature, such as Flammer’s (2015) comparison of narrowly passed and narrowly failed proposals and Kahn et al.’s (2023) natural experiment with public pension funds. Its weaknesses are cost and scale. It requires staff, expertise, and often collaboration, and the strongest evidence comes from investors with unusual resources or mandates. It can also involve trade-offs for shareholders, as the decline in profitability reported by Naaraayanan et al. (2026) shows. Still, of the three strategies, it is the one most likely to change what firms actually do.

## ***8.3 Implications for Investors and Policymakers***

These findings have practical implications. Asset owners such as pension funds and endowments should ask their managers for records of engagement and its results, not just the ESG scores of their portfolios. A low-carbon portfolio says little about whether the manager has reduced anyone’s emissions. Owners considering divestment should weigh what they give up, because once they sell, they lose the vote and the access to management that engagement relies on (Kahn et al., 2023). If they do divest, a conditional policy that promises to reinvest after verified reform is more likely to change behavior than permanent exclusion, as long as the promise is credible (Edmans, Levit, & Schneemeier, 2025).

For fund managers and index providers, Hartzmark and Shue’s (2026) critique of percentage-based targets is directly relevant. Measuring progress in absolute reductions, and concentrating attention on the highest emitters, would direct effort to where impact elasticity is largest.

For policymakers, the banking evidence points in two directions. Targeted exit from specific asset classes, such as coal, appears able to change real outcomes (Green & Vallée, 2025), while broad pledges may mainly change reporting (Kacperczyk & Peydró, 2022). Better and more standardized disclosure would help investors tell real improvement from relabeling and would reduce the rating disagreement documented by Gibson Brandon et al. (2021). Finally, because tighter financial constraints are associated with more pollution, especially where enforcement is weak (Xu & Kim, 2022), policies that restrict financing to brown firms should be paired with strong enforcement, so that constrained firms cannot save money by cutting abatement.

# **9. Limitations and Future Research**

The literature has several gaps. First, much of the strongest engagement evidence comes from a single asset manager or a specific group of investors (Dimson et al., 2015; Kahn et al., 2023). Most ESG-minded investors lack the scale, expertise, or political mandate of these investors, so the results may not generalize.

Second, Hartzmark and Shue (2026) is recent and has not yet been widely tested. Future work should check whether the asymmetry holds for social and governance outcomes, in other time periods, and outside the United States.

Third, ESG rating disagreement (Gibson Brandon et al., 2021) means that some studies may be measuring noise in the ratings rather than sustainability. Better disclosure standards, or research designs that account for disagreement across raters, would help.

A related problem is how impact itself is measured. Some studies use total emissions, others use emissions per unit of sales or output, and others use ESG scores, and these measures can move in different directions. Akey and Appel (2019) illustrate the point: total emissions at targeted plants fell by 17 percent, but most of that came from lower output, so the improvement per unit was much smaller. Most studies also track only a firm’s own direct emissions. If investor pressure leads firms to sell dirty assets to owners who face less scrutiny, the firm’s reported emissions fall while total pollution does not. Kahn et al. (2023) find no evidence of this in their setting, but it remains a concern for the wider literature, and studies that follow assets after they change hands would be valuable.

Fourth, the divestment and engagement literatures have developed largely separately. Gantchev et al. (2022) is one of the few studies that tests how exit threats and engagement interact within the same firms. More work of this kind would show when each tool, or a combination of the two, works best.

Finally, almost all of these studies use U.S. or other developed-market data. Much of the world’s pollution comes from emerging markets, where capital markets are thinner, ownership is more concentrated, and disclosure is weaker. Financial constraints are also likely to bind more tightly there, which, given the evidence in Section 5, could make withdrawing capital even more harmful. Whether these findings carry over is an open and important question.

# **10. Conclusion**

We set out to ask whether sustainable investing changes how firms behave or only how their shares are priced. Organizing 35 studies around a two-link chain gives a fairly clear answer. Sustainable investing does move prices and financing costs, though usually by small amounts in diversified equity markets. Changes in financing costs do much less to change behavior. Our anchor study suggests that the most common strategy, moving capital from brown firms to green ones, may increase pollution by squeezing brown firms while doing little for green ones (Hartzmark & Shue, 2026). Related work on financial constraints, fund selection, and bank lending supports this view.

Engagement has a much better record. Studies of a specialist fund, shareholder votes, index managers, hedge funds, and public pension funds find real improvements, including lower emissions, after investors press management for change, and one natural experiment suggests that divesting gives up that influence altogether (Kahn et al., 2023). Exit still has a role, but mainly as a credible threat that strengthens engagement rather than as a strategy on its own.

For investors who want their capital to change corporate behavior, the practical lesson is that how they invest matters more than how much they label as sustainable. The evidence reviewed here suggests that staying invested and engaging, potentially backed by a credible threat to exit, offers a more promising route to real corporate change than unconditional divestment alone.
